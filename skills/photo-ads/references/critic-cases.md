# Critic cases

For the critic only: the maker never reads this file. Each case is an ad its owner turned down, described so it stands on its own: the business, the offer, the words and the scene, then what was wrong. The brands are invented and the cases are retold from real reviews. The cases show taste. They are not rules to apply. For each ad, also say what in it makes a stranger want what it sells.

1. **A busy pattern behind everything.** Lumbrook Coffee, a small speciality roastery that sells its beans and runs a coffee bar. Headline "Roasted on Mondays, in your cup by Friday", supporting line "Order beans by post or drink them at the bar." A set of five ads put a small, dense hand-painted pattern over the whole ground of every ad, behind the photo of the bag and behind the words. The pattern was too small and too busy: it took the eye away from the headline, the photo and the action. A set the owner liked used one large-scale pattern in one place, with a dark panel against a light one. What the owner looks for is contrast: calm against lively, dark against light.

2. **Mess read as realism.** The same roastery, with the headline "Our house blend, now in the corner shop" and no supporting line, in generated product scenes: a shop window, a hand taking a bag from a grocer's shelf, a flat lay of two bags. Critics preferred the versions with crumbs, a coffee ring, a half-drunk cup and a shopper in the aisle, and called them more real. The owner chose the tidy version every time: crumbs and a half-drunk cup look careless, not real. The owner also caught a brown logo on the shop-window glass, which reads poorly on glass.

3. **Light with no source.** The same roastery, headline "New: the pink bag", supporting line "A light roast from Ethiopia, while it lasts." A generated photo of a pink-patterned coffee bag on a counter. A bright glow ran along the bag's edge that no lamp in the scene could explain. It made the photo look generated.

4. **A house icon on a church.** Harrow & Finch, an estate agent in a harbour city. Headline "We know your street", no supporting line, a two-tone photo of the hilltop church every local knows. A drawn map pin with a house icon in it sat on the church spire. A critic read it as "a property location on a local landmark" and counted it as carrying the message. It says the church is for sale. A pin on a home could work. Drawn icons and pictograms of this kind make an ad look fake unless they are handled with great care.

5. **A subject that does not belong in a feed.** Pettlewick Flowers, a florist. Headline "Funeral wreaths bound the way you ask", supporting line "We bring the wreath to the church or chapel before the service." The ad was made for a social feed and aimed at relatives. Every critic judged its words and picture; none asked whether a funeral belongs in a feed at all. Someone who has just lost a person and then sees a funeral ad in their feed may feel watched.

6. **Safe enough to pass, too basic to sell.** The same florist, a graduation bouquet ad for a story feed: a close photo of a blue and yellow bouquet in the top half, then a cream panel with a small serif headline "Graduation bouquets in blue and yellow", the supporting line "Order by Thursday, collect on the day" and the logo, and an empty bottom quarter. It passed three critic loops and a judge, because nothing in it was wrong. It was the most basic layout there is, with nothing a designer would be proud of. A bolder version let the photo fill the ad and set the headline three times larger in the colours the words name.
